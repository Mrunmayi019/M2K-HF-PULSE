\begin{abstract}
Home monitoring gives heart-failure (HF) patients daily measurements but no physiological interpretation. We present HF-PULSE, an open-source, population-parameterized physiological model (a ``digital twin'' in a limited sense) that interprets daily wearable trends through a mechanistic physiology engine. A Random Forest reads a 21-day window of six daily signals and routine clinical values and estimates a deterioration scenario and its severity; these drive the Kitware Pulse engine, whose simulated hemodynamics feed a clinically anchored, interpretable risk score and a three-level alert. In a continuous mode the twin carries the engine's state from day to day, and a resumed run's outputs match an uninterrupted run's to 15 significant figures, provided the twin re-issues disease modifiers on resume. We evaluated the twin with outcomes committed to version control before any run: ten scripted 21-day patient stories, development and held-out seeds, and a replication on new seeds. An alert guard built on the risk score's hemodynamic components, selected on development seeds, cut false alerts by 68\% there and by 61\% on held-out seeds without losing a detection. Re-running with the cohort's ejection fraction inside the training range (with two minor protocol changes) doubled day-by-day label accuracy (17.6\% to 37.1\%), raised detection from 21 to 24 of 30 deteriorating series and lowered false alerts from 8.7 to 4.8 per 100 patient-days; the results met six of the eight outcome predictions we committed before the runs. In both experiments, high risk arose only after the classifier's first exertion label, and on held-out seeds a classifier threshold selected on development seeds detected more series (15 vs.\ 10 of 15) at the same false-alert rate. The engine did not improve detection; it adds a hemodynamic breakdown of each alert, whose clinical usefulness remains untested, and we identify the changes needed to go further. The pipeline ran end to end on real home data from 13 of 16 HF patients; on 17,129 MIMIC-IV admissions, the only component computable without wearables, a monotone function of admission MAP, reached AUC 0.596, similar to age alone.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%=====================================================================
\subsection{Health challenge}
Heart failure affected an estimated 64.3 million people worldwide in 2017 \cite{james2018gbd,savarese2023burden} and is defined by progression: patients decline over days to weeks rather than instantaneously. Current guidelines emphasize early recognition of congestion and decompensation \cite{heidenreich2022}.

Yet three structural gaps remain in practice. First, monitoring is episodic: patients are typically seen every few weeks, and a patient can move from stable to crisis between visits. Second, consumer wearables report numbers, not physiology: a rising resting heart rate is measured, but whether it reflects benign variation or an early compensatory response to falling cardiac output is left to the patient. Third, threshold alarms (e.g., SpO$_2<92\%$) fire only after a value is already abnormal; they describe where a patient is, not where they are heading.

\subsection{The digital-twin approach}
Remote monitoring has shown benefit when it captures physiology rather than symptoms alone, from structured telemonitoring \cite{inglis2008telemonitoring} to implanted pulmonary-artery pressure sensors \cite{abraham2011champion}, and multi-sensor wearables combined with machine learning have predicted HF hospitalization days in advance \cite{stehlik2020linkhf,gautam2022wearables}. These data-driven predictors, however, offer little mechanistic explanation of \emph{why} an alert fired.

Cardiac digital twins promise exactly that mechanistic layer \cite{corral2020twin,coorey2022twin,niederer2021scaling}, but most are built from imaging or invasive measurements fitted to one patient at a time \cite{gu2025hftwin,geddes2025rhftwin}, and few are evaluated over weeks of everyday data.

We report a case study of a twin that sits between these two traditions: it takes daily home-monitoring trends (wearable and home-device signals) as input, interprets them through a mechanistic whole-body physiology engine (Kitware Pulse \cite{bray2019pulse}), and produces interpretable risk, staging and forward projections. We use ``twin'' in a limited sense: population statistics parameterize the engine body, and the patient's ejection fraction and estimated severity scale it; we do not fit it to the individual.

\subsection{Research methodology}\label{sec:overview}
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the pipeline. A patient record holds demographics and a clinical report (ejection fraction, NT-proBNP); daily wearable readings accumulate until they fill a 21-day window, which triggers one background assessment: (1) the scenario classifier and severity regressor (the ``scenario model''); (2) construction of a Pulse patient and scenario file; (3) simulation inside the Pulse container with crash and timeout detection; (4) hemodynamic feature extraction; (5) risk scoring, NYHA staging and trend-based deterioration rate; and (6) three further Pulse runs at projected severities for 7, 14 and 30 days. Section~\ref{sec:methods} details each stage, Section~\ref{sec:engineering} the engine problems we solved, Section~\ref{sec:results} the evaluation on synthetic patients and Section~\ref{sec:validation} the tests against real data.

\subsection{Contributions}
Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A complete, open-source pipeline (wearable trends $\rightarrow$ ML scenario and severity estimation $\rightarrow$ Pulse simulation $\rightarrow$ interpretable risk scoring, staging and projection), deployed as a service with a dashboard and an optional continuous mode that carries engine state from day to day, with resumed outputs matching uninterrupted runs (Sections~\ref{sec:overview} and~\ref{sec:methods}).
\item Engine-level findings for using Pulse as an HF twin: mapping ejection fraction onto the engine, a root-caused instability boundary when disease modifiers and exertion combine, and a state save/resume method whose outputs match uninterrupted runs (Section~\ref{sec:engineering}).
\item A pre-specified, replicated evaluation (Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:validation}): a 21-day scenario test with development and held-out seeds, a replication on new seeds after a predicted fix, an ablation against the classifier alone, a label-only rule and a clinical weight rule, and real-patient and real-outcome tests. It shows what the physiology layer adds (an alert guard built on its hemodynamic components that cut false alerts by 61--68\%, and a mechanistic account of every alert), shows that every high-risk day followed an exertion label that switched on simulated exercise, and shows that a tuned classifier detected more deteriorating series at the same false-alert rate.
\item A reproducible research artifact: frozen, hash-checked models, 353 automated test cases passing in continuous integration, research features behind default-off flags with a golden-file parity test, and a fresh-clone reproduction of the demonstration patients.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Literature Survey}\label{sec:related}
%=====================================================================
We group prior work into five themes: digital twins in healthcare and cardiology, physiology engines and lumped cardiovascular models, wearable and implanted HF monitoring, HF risk scores and machine learning, and the validation of digital twins. We then position HF-PULSE against them and state the gaps we address.

\subsection{Digital twins in healthcare and cardiology}\label{sec:rel-twins}
Venkatesh \textit{et al.}\ \cite{venkatesh2022hdt} define a health digital twin as a virtual representation of a patient generated from multimodal patient data, population data and real-time updates, and name computation, clinical implementation, data governance and product oversight as the main considerations for translation. Balasubramanyam \textit{et al.}\ \cite{balasubramanyam2024review} surveyed the 2020--2023 literature on digital twins in healthcare: their roles and benefits, the layers needed to implement a twin-based smart healthcare system, and its limitations and challenges. Katsoulakis \textit{et al.}\ \cite{katsoulakis2024dt4h} concluded in a scoping review that digital twins for health are still at an early stage.

In cardiology, Corral-Acero \textit{et al.}\ \cite{corral2020twin} framed the digital twin as the route to precision cardiology, Coorey \textit{et al.}\ \cite{coorey2022twin} reviewed the emerging health-twin field for cardiovascular disease, and Niederer \textit{et al.}\ \cite{niederer2021scaling} argued that twins must move from artisanal, single-patient builds to scalable, industrial pipelines.

Recent HF-specific twins fit mechanistic cardiovascular models to measured data. Gu \textit{et al.}\ \cite{gu2025hftwin} fitted twins to 343 HF patients and used the fitted parameters for interpretable phenotyping and prognosis. Geddes \textit{et al.}\ \cite{geddes2025rhftwin} built CFD-based twins that non-invasively estimate markers of right HF. Balasubramanyam \textit{et al.}\ \cite{balasubramanyam2025twin} proposed a cardiovascular twin combining time-varying compliance, Fourier-based ECG reconstruction and 3D anatomical modeling.

\subsection{Physiology engines and lumped cardiovascular models}\label{sec:rel-engines}
Zero-dimensional (lumped-parameter) models represent the components of the cardiovascular system in simplified, lumped form. Shi \textit{et al.}\ \cite{shi2011review} reviewed them as a concise way to evaluate hemodynamic interactions among cardiovascular organs, with one-dimensional models adding pulse-wave transmission at far lower cost than three-dimensional CFD. The arterial Windkessel is the classic lumped element: Frank's two-element form, a resistance and a compliance, explains diastolic pressure decay, and a third element, the characteristic impedance, improves its behavior in systole; as a lumped model it cannot represent spatially distributed phenomena, but it approximates ventricular afterload simply and fairly accurately \cite{westerhof2009windkessel}. For the heart itself, Suga and Sagawa \cite{suga1974elastance} showed in the excised canine left ventricle that the instantaneous pressure--volume relationship is approximated by a time-varying elastance, $P(t)=E(t)\,[V(t)-V_d]$, the concept on which the elastance models in Section~\ref{sec:engine} build.

Three open-source engines package such models at whole-body or whole-circulation scale. Pulse \cite{bray2019pulse,pulse_circuit_methodology} and BioGears \cite{baird2020biogears} both model physiology with electric-circuit analogues; BioGears targets medical education, research and training and supports in-silico trials \cite{baird2020biogears}. CircAdapt \cite{arts2005circadapt} simulates the beat-to-beat dynamics of the four-chamber heart with systemic and pulmonary circulations, and lets cardiac and vascular tissue adapt to mechanical load through local rules. We build on Pulse, whose open-source engine accepts actions and conditions during a run and can serialize and resume its full state (Section~\ref{sec:engineering}).

\subsection{Wearable and implanted HF monitoring}\label{sec:rel-monitoring}
Non-invasive telemonitoring trials have mixed results. Tele-HF \cite{chaudhry2010telehf} randomized 1,653 recently hospitalized patients to daily telephone-reported symptoms and weight or usual care and found no significant difference in readmission or death within 180 days (52.3\% vs.\ 51.5\%). BEAT-HF \cite{ong2016beathf} combined health-coaching calls with telemonitoring of blood pressure, heart rate, symptoms and weight in 1,437 patients and did not reduce 180-day readmissions (50.8\% vs.\ 49.2\%; adjusted HR 1.03). TIM-HF2 \cite{koehler2018timhf2}, a structured remote patient management program in 1,571 patients, reduced the percentage of days lost to unplanned cardiovascular admission or all-cause death (4.88\% vs.\ 6.64\%; ratio 0.80, 95\% CI 0.65--1.00) and all-cause mortality (HR 0.70). Earlier structured telephone support and telemonitoring programs were reviewed by Inglis \textit{et al.}\ \cite{inglis2008telemonitoring}.

Implanted sensors measure physiology more directly. In CHAMPION \cite{abraham2011champion}, management guided by a wireless pulmonary-artery pressure sensor lowered the 6-month rate of HF hospitalization (0.31 vs.\ 0.44; HR 0.70). GUIDE-HF \cite{lindenfeld2021guidehf} extended this approach to NYHA class II--IV patients, and its primary endpoint, a composite of mortality and total HF events at 12 months, was not significantly reduced (HR 0.88, 95\% CI 0.74--1.05). The HeartLogic index from the MultiSENSE study \cite{boehmer2017multisense} combines heart sounds, respiration, thoracic impedance, heart rate and activity from implanted CRT defibrillators; in its sequestered test cohort it detected 70\% of HF events with 1.47 unexplained alerts per patient-year and a median lead time of 34 days. MultiSENSE reported unexplained alerts per patient-year, the device-study analogue of our false alerts per 100 patient-days.

Wearable predictors aim for similar performance without an implant. The LINK-HF study \cite{stehlik2020linkhf} used a disposable multisensor chest patch and a machine-learned personalized baseline: deviations from the baseline detected precursors of HF rehospitalization with 76--88\% sensitivity and 85\% specificity, and the median time from first alert to readmission was 6.5 days. Gautam \textit{et al.}\ \cite{gautam2022wearables} surveyed AI, wearables and remote monitoring in HF.

\subsection{HF risk scores and machine learning}\label{sec:rel-risk}
The Seattle Heart Failure Model \cite{levy2006shfm} was derived in 1,125 patients with a Cox model and validated in five cohorts totalling 9,942 patients, with an overall AUC of 0.729 for survival. The MAGGIC score \cite{pocock2013maggic} is a widely used, externally validated HF survival score derived from 39,372 patients in 30 studies, and the LACE index \cite{vanwalraven2010lace} is a general, not HF-specific, score for early death or unplanned readmission after discharge; we use both as external benchmarks (Section~\ref{sec:outcomes}). Across 117 HF prediction models in 55 papers, Ouwerkerk \textit{et al.}\ \cite{ouwerkerk2014models} found a mean C-statistic of 0.66 (0.71 for mortality, 0.68 for HF hospitalization) and judged prediction, especially of HF hospitalization, only moderately successful.

Machine learning has improved these figures modestly. On the Tele-HF trial data, Mortazavi \textit{et al.}\ \cite{mortazavi2016ml} found that random forests raised the C-statistic for 30-day all-cause readmission from 0.533 (logistic regression) to 0.628, and boosting raised it for HF readmission from 0.543 to 0.678. Chicco and Jurman \cite{chicco2020hf} predicted survival from serum creatinine and ejection fraction alone. None of these models simulates the physiology that produces the risk.

\subsection{Validating digital twins}\label{sec:rel-validation}
A twin's predictions need evidence of credibility before they inform care. Viceconti \textit{et al.}\ \cite{viceconti2021insilico} describe a framework for assessing the credibility of mechanistic (``biophysical'') models used in the regulatory evaluation of medical products, built on verification, validation and uncertainty quantification and beginning with the definition of the model's context of use. Pathmanathan and Gray \cite{pathmanathan2018validation} note that credibility is established primarily by validation against experimental or clinical data, but that for complex multiscale models it often rests on many distinct kinds of evidence, so the extent to which such a model can be trusted is often unclear.

Pre-specification guards the evaluation itself. Nosek \textit{et al.}\ \cite{nosek2018prereg} argue that defining the research questions and analysis plan before observing outcomes separates predictions from postdictions, and describe strategies for cases that fall short of the ideal, such as preexisting data. Our scenario tests follow this logic with outcomes committed to version control before any run, which is weaker than registration in a public registry (Section~\ref{sec:exp1}).

\subsection{Positioning and gaps addressed}\label{sec:rel-gaps}
Table~\ref{tab:related} contrasts these approaches. Mechanistic twins personalize deeply but require hospital measurements; wearable predictors scale but are opaque. Our twin trades per-patient parameter fitting for deployability from home data, and adds a mechanistic layer that wearable-only predictors lack. We claim novelty for the integrated pipeline and its evaluation, not for any single component. Specifically, we address four gaps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Input data.} The HF twins in Section~\ref{sec:rel-twins} are built from imaging, echocardiography or invasive measurements; we drive a mechanistic engine from daily wearable trends and routine clinical values.
\item \emph{Explanation.} Wearable and implanted predictors (Section~\ref{sec:rel-monitoring}) output an index or an alert; we attach a hemodynamic breakdown to each alert, whose clinical usefulness remains untested.
\item \emph{State.} We carry the engine's physiological state from day to day instead of rebuilding the patient for each assessment (Section~\ref{sec:engineering}).
\item \emph{Evaluation.} We evaluate over 21-day trajectories against outcomes committed before the runs, on development and held-out seeds and a replication, and report false-alert burden per patient-day as device studies do.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
%=====================================================================
\subsection{Reference data and synthetic training population}\label{sec:data}
We used no real patient data for training. Instead, we took the generator's population statistics from aggregate statistics of real sources (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}): 11,837 MIMIC-IV HF admissions \cite{johnson2023mimiciv} for age and sex (the extract also gives laboratory reference values, which the generator does not yet use); the Chicco--Jurman HF clinical records \cite{chicco2020hf}, the only source with ejection fraction, for the HFrEF and HFpEF profiles; NHANES for height, weight and BMI by sex; and published age-specific NT-proBNP cut-offs \cite{januzzi2006icon}. The remaining parameters are documented assumptions: the healthy EF profile, the wearable baselines, the scenario changes $\Delta_{v,c}$ (Table~\ref{tab:deltas}) and the coefficients that link EF, severity and NT-proBNP. The repository stores only aggregate statistics from the real sources; raw files remain outside version control.

We generated $n=2{,}000$ synthetic patients across five scenarios. Each patient receives a scenario $c$ and a severity
\begin{equation}
\sigma=\begin{cases}0.15\,\xi, & c=\text{stable},\\ \xi, & \text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad \xi\sim U(0,1),
\label{eq:severity}
\end{equation}
so stable patients' severity is scaled into $[0,\,0.15]$.

\subsubsection{Clinical state}
The generator draws ejection fraction from the scenario's EF profile $p(c)$ (healthy for stable; HFpEF for deconditioning and cardiac stress; HFrEF for fluid overload and acute deterioration) and lowers it by $12\sigma$ points:
\begin{equation}
\text{EF}=\operatorname{clip}\!\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu_p,\,(w_p/4)^2)-12\sigma,\;15,\;75\big),
\label{eq:ef}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_p$ and $w_p$ are the profile's mean and range width (HFrEF: 32.3\%, range 14--40\%; HFpEF: 56.9\%, range 50--80\%; healthy: 62\%, range 55--70\%). A mix of EF deficit and severity, scaled to age-specific diagnostic cut-offs, drives NT-proBNP, so BNP falls with EF and rises with severity:
\begin{align}
d_{\text{EF}}&=\operatorname{clip}\!\big((70-\text{EF})/55,\;0,\;1\big),\label{eq:efdeficit}\\
b&=\operatorname{clip}\!\big(0.6\,d_{\text{EF}}+0.4\,\sigma,\;0,\;1\big),\\
\text{NT-proBNP}&=k(a)\,(0.25+2b)\,\eta,\qquad \ln\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.25^2),
\end{align}
where $k(a)$ is the age-specific NT-proBNP cut-off for acute HF: 450, 900 and 1,800\,pg/mL for ages below 50, 50--75 and above 75 years \cite{januzzi2006icon}. The lognormal factor $\eta$ keeps BNP positive and right-skewed.

\subsubsection{Wearable trends}
Each wearable signal $v$ on day $t$ then follows
\begin{equation}
v(t)=b_v+\Delta_{v,c}\,\sigma\,g_m(t/20)+\epsilon_{v,t},
\label{eq:wearable}
\end{equation}
where $b_v$ is a patient baseline (drawn from population statistics, or the patient's own weight), $\Delta_{v,c}$ is the maximum change for scenario $c$ (Table~\ref{tab:deltas}; e.g., fluid overload: $+4.5$\,kg weight, $+12$\,bpm resting HR, $-2.5$ points SpO$_2$), $g_m$ is a progression curve for mode $m$,
\begin{equation}
g_m(u)=\begin{cases}u, & \text{deteriorating},\ c\neq\text{AD},\\ u^2, & \text{deteriorating},\ c=\text{AD},\\ \max(0,\,1-1.15u), & \text{recovering},\\ 0, & \text{stable},\end{cases}
\label{eq:progression}
\end{equation}
with AD denoting acute deterioration, and $\epsilon_{v,t}$ is independent Gaussian noise with SD equal to 15\% of the population SD (0.4\,kg for weight). Over the 21-day window $u=t/20$ runs from 0 to 1, so a deteriorating patient reaches the full change $\Delta_{v,c}\sigma$ on the last day. Signals are therefore correlated only through the shared $\sigma$ and $g_m$, not through any physiological coupling. Stable patients are always in the stable mode; for the other scenarios the generator chooses the deteriorating mode with probability $0.4+0.5\sigma$ (capped at 0.95) and the recovering mode otherwise. Finally, it clips SpO$_2$ at 100\%, steps and sleep at zero and HRV at 1\,ms.

\subsection{Scenario model: classifier and severity regressor}
Two Random Forests \cite{breiman2001rf} (300 trees, default depth) share one 29-column feature matrix: five clinical features (age, sex, BMI, EF, NT-proBNP) and, for each of six wearable signals, the first-7-day mean, last-7-day mean, their difference and the 21-day linear slope (24 features). We drop the trend-mode column used during synthesis to prevent label leakage, and split patients 70/15/15 (stratified by scenario) into training, validation and test sets.

\subsection{Physiology engine and personalization}\label{sec:engine}
\subsubsection{Circuit model}
Pulse models the circulation as a lumped-parameter network, the hydraulic analogue of an electric circuit: nodes carry pressures, paths carry flows, vessel segments are resistors and elastic segments are capacitors, so that for each segment
\begin{equation}
Q=\frac{\Delta P}{R},\qquad C=\frac{dV}{dP},
\label{eq:circuit}
\end{equation}
with arterial segments represented as Windkessel elements (a compliance in parallel with a resistance) \cite{pulse_cv_methodology}. Pulse assembles these branch equations with Kirchhoff's laws and solves the network by modified nodal analysis at every time step \cite{pulse_circuit_methodology}. At a compliant node, conservation of volume and~(\ref{eq:circuit}) with constant $C$ give
\begin{equation}
C\,\frac{dP}{dt}=\frac{dV}{dt}=\sum Q_{\text{in}}-\sum Q_{\text{out}}.
\label{eq:node}
\end{equation}
For a single two-element Windkessel fed by an inflow $Q(t)$ and draining through $R$ to a downstream pressure taken as zero, (\ref{eq:node}) becomes
\begin{equation}
C\,\frac{dP}{dt}=Q(t)-\frac{P}{R},
\label{eq:windkessel}
\end{equation}
so that when inflow stops in diastole the pressure decays as $P(t)=P_0\,e^{-t/RC}$, the behavior Frank's model was introduced to explain \cite{westerhof2009windkessel}.

\subsubsection{Heart model}
Each heart chamber is a time-varying compliance whose inverse, the elastance, follows a model adapted from Stergiopulos \textit{et al.}\ \cite{stergiopulos1996}:
\begin{equation}
E_v(t)=\left(E_{\max,v}-E_{\min,v}\right)\frac{f(t)}{f_{\max}}+E_{\min,v},
\label{eq:elastance}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
f(t)=\frac{\left(t/\alpha_1T\right)^{n_1}}{1+\left(t/\alpha_1T\right)^{n_1}}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\left(t/\alpha_2T\right)^{n_2}},
\label{eq:hill}
\end{equation}
where $E_{\max,v}$ and $E_{\min,v}$ are the maximum and minimum ventricular elastances, $T$ is the cardiac-cycle duration and $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,n_1,n_2$ are shape parameters; chamber compliance is $1/E_v(t)$. This is the time-varying-elastance description of the ventricle \cite{suga1974elastance} in circuit form. The cyclic change in compliance produces the pressure and volume changes that drive flow through the network, and Pulse computes stroke volume, cardiac output and arterial pressures from the circuit quantities at each time step. Ejection fraction is therefore an \emph{output} of this model, not an input.

\subsubsection{How the engine applies modifiers}
We therefore act on the parameters of~(\ref{eq:circuit})--(\ref{eq:hill}). In Pulse 4.3.1 the \code{CardiovascularMechanicsModification} multipliers and the \code{ChronicVentricularSystolicDysfunction} condition enter at the start of every cardiac cycle as \cite{pulse_source431}
\begin{align}
E_{\max,L}&\leftarrow \beta_E\,k_{\text{HF}}\,m_{\text{SV}}\,E^{0}_{\max,L},\label{eq:emax}\\
E_{\max,R}&\leftarrow \beta_E\,m_{\text{SV}}\,E^{0}_{\max,R},\\
f_{\text{HR}}&\leftarrow m_{\text{HR}}\,f_{\text{HR}},\qquad R_{i}\leftarrow m_{R}\,R_{i},\label{eq:hrmod}\\
C_{i}&\leftarrow m_{C}\,C_{i},\qquad\ \ C_{j}\leftarrow m_{V}\,C_{j},\label{eq:cmod}
\end{align}
where $E^{0}_{\max}$ are the configured maximum elastances, $\beta_E$ is the baroreflex elastance scale (1 with the reflex off), $k_{\text{HF}}=0.27$ when the condition is present and 1 otherwise, $f_{\text{HR}}$ is the heart-rate driver frequency (after baroreflex, chemoreflex and drug effects, and bounded by the patient's minimum and maximum heart rate), $i$ runs over systemic resistance and compliance paths and $j$ over venous compliance paths. The engine squares $m_{\text{SV}}$ when it exceeds 1, which our mapping never does. The stroke-volume multiplier therefore acts on contractility (maximum elastance), not on stroke volume directly, and the condition is a fixed 73\% cut in left-ventricular maximum elastance with no severity parameter. Fig.~\ref{fig:circuit} shows where each lever acts on the circulation.

\subsubsection{EF-to-engine mapping}
We map EF and severity onto these multipliers and, for reduced EF, the binary \code{ChronicVentricularSystolicDysfunction} condition (the fixed elastance reduction $k_{\text{HF}}$). With $d_{\text{EF}}$ from~(\ref{eq:efdeficit}), the combined deficit is
\begin{equation}
x=\begin{cases}0.5\,\sigma, & \text{EF}\le40\%,\\ \max\big(d_{\text{EF}},\,0.8\,\sigma\big), & \text{EF}>40\%,\end{cases}
\label{eq:deficit}
\end{equation}
where the condition is applied ($k_{\text{HF}}=0.27$) in the first case and not ($k_{\text{HF}}=1$) in the second. The base multipliers are
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
m_{\text{SV}}=\max(0.5,\,1-0.5x),\\
m_{R}=m_{C}=\max(0.6,\,1-0.3x).
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:basemods}
\end{equation}
When we apply the condition, the continuous multiplier represents only the \emph{additional} acute severity (the first case of~(\ref{eq:deficit})), because stacking both produced cardiovascular collapse in validation. Since $x\le1$, $m_{\text{SV}}\ge0.5$ and $m_R=m_C\ge0.7$; with the condition, $x\le0.5$, so the left maximum elastance stays at or above $0.27\times0.75\approx0.20$ of its configured value before baroreflex scaling.

\subsubsection{Scenario actions}
In the default fresh mode, the four non-stable scenarios add their own multipliers to the same action; a multiplier named by both the scenario and~(\ref{eq:basemods}) takes the scenario's value:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{FO:}\quad & m_V=\max(0.5,\,1-0.4\sigma),\\
\text{CS:}\quad & m_{\text{HR}}=1+0.3\sigma,\quad e=\sigma,\\
\text{DC:}\quad & m_R=m_C=\max(0.85,\,1-0.1\sigma),\\
\text{AD:}\quad & m_V=\max(0.4,\,1-0.5\sigma),\\
& m_{\text{HR}}=1+0.4\sigma,\quad e=0.6\sigma,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:scenarioacts}
\end{equation}
with the scenario abbreviations of Table~\ref{tab:deltas}, where $e$ is the intensity of an \code{Exercise} action, clipped to $[0.1,\,0.5]$; Section~\ref{sec:engineering} shows that the intensity the engine tolerates under disease modifiers is patient-specific and can be lower. A fresh run starts from the patient file and condition, stabilizes for 60\,s, applies the actions and advances 10\,min.

\subsubsection{Engine boundary}
Pulse rejects patients outside age 18--65 and BMI 16--30. We clamp demographics only at the engine boundary, so the patient's true EF and severity still drive the simulation while the simulated body is a proxy:
\begin{equation}
a'=\operatorname{clip}(a,\,18,\,65),\qquad w'=\operatorname{clip}\big(w,\;16.5\,h^2,\;29.5\,h^2\big),
\label{eq:clamp}
\end{equation}
with height $h$ (m) held fixed; the half-unit BMI margins avoid rejections from floating-point rounding.

\subsection{Hemodynamic features and risk score}
From the first and last samples of each run we extract HR rise, MAP drop, cardiac-output (CO) drop, a compensation flag (stroke volume held $\geq95\%$) and an instability flag (MAP$_{\text{end}}<65$\,mmHg):
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\Delta\text{HR}=\text{HR}_{\text{end}}-\text{HR}_{\text{start}},\qquad \Delta\text{MAP}=\text{MAP}_{\text{start}}-\text{MAP}_{\text{end}},\\
\Delta\text{CO}=100\,\frac{\text{CO}_{\text{start}}-\text{CO}_{\text{end}}}{\text{CO}_{\text{start}}},\\
F_{\text{comp}}=\mathbf{1}\!\left[\frac{\text{SV}_{\text{end}}}{\text{SV}_{\text{start}}}\ge0.95\right],\qquad F_{\text{inst}}=\mathbf{1}\!\left[\text{MAP}_{\text{end}}<65\right].
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:features}
\end{equation}
In fresh mode and on a patient's first continuous day, the run includes stabilization, so these deltas span the healthy baseline to the end of the encounter; on later continuous days they span only that day's encounter, from the re-issued modifiers to its end (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

The primary risk score (Table~\ref{tab:riskscore}) normalizes each to $[0,1]$ against a clinically anchored full scale and forms a weighted sum:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
s_{\text{HR}}=\tfrac{1}{3}N_2(70+\Delta\text{HR}),\qquad s_{\text{MAP}}=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{\Delta\text{MAP}}{27.5},0,1\right),\\
s_{\text{CO}}=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{\Delta\text{CO}}{30},0,1\right),\\
s_{\text{comp}}=1-F_{\text{comp}},\qquad s_{\text{inst}}=F_{\text{inst}},\\
A=0.15\,s_{\text{HR}}+0.20\,s_{\text{MAP}}+0.20\,s_{\text{CO}}\\
+0.15\,s_{\text{comp}}+0.30\,s_{\text{inst}},
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:acute}
\end{equation}
where $A=\sum_i w_i s_i$ is the acute score, $N_2(h)$ is the NEWS2 heart-rate score \cite{rcp2017news2} (3 for $h\le40$, 1 for $40<h\le50$, 0 for $50<h\le90$, 1 for $90<h\le110$, 2 for $110<h\le130$ and 3 above 130\,bpm), applied to an assumed resting HR of 70\,bpm plus the rise, and $27.5=92.5-65$\,mmHg spans the healthy anchor to the instability threshold.

During validation the score was structurally blind to fluid overload, whose danger appears as an already-congested baseline rather than an acute change, so we added a sixth input:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
r=\max\big(\textstyle\sum_{i} w_i\, s_i,\; s_{\text{baseline}}(\text{MAP}_{\text{start}})\big),\\
s_{\text{baseline}}=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{92.5-\text{MAP}_{\text{start}}}{27.5},0,1\right),
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:risk}
\end{equation}
where $s_i$ are the five acute sub-scores and $s_{\text{baseline}}$ scores how far the simulated starting MAP lies below a healthy 92.5\,mmHg anchor. We call a day's dominant mechanism \emph{baseline} when $s_{\text{baseline}}>A$ and \emph{acute} otherwise; the C3 guard uses this label (Algorithm~\ref{alg:alert}). Buckets are LOW ($r<0.35$), MODERATE ($r<0.65$) and HIGH; these cut-points are an engineering choice, not a clinical citation.

Two consequences follow from~(\ref{eq:acute}) and~(\ref{eq:risk}). First, no two acute components can reach HIGH: the two largest weights sum to 0.50, so $A\ge0.65$ requires at least three non-zero components. Second, the baseline term alone gives MODERATE when MAP$_{\text{start}}\le82.9$\,mmHg and HIGH when MAP$_{\text{start}}\le74.6$\,mmHg.

We later found that the fluid-overload scenario itself is mis-specified (Section~\ref{sec:limits}): it lowers venous compliance without adding circulating volume. The baseline-deficit term therefore compensates for a flawed scenario rather than reflecting correctly simulated congestion, and its improvement on the batch (30/30 LOW to 29/30 MODERATE) should be read in that light.

We keep an XGBoost regressor \cite{chen2016xgboost} on the same features as a secondary comparison only (5-fold CV MAE $0.089\pm0.020$, $R^2$ $0.828\pm0.091$; $n=117$ is too small to make it primary).

\subsection{Staging, deterioration rate and projection}
NYHA staging is rule-based: AHA/ACC structural criteria (LVEF $\leq40\%$ or age-adjusted NT-proBNP above cut-off) \cite{heidenreich2022} gate whether symptoms have a structural basis, and the simulated exertion response places the patient in classes I--IV.

The system computes a deterioration rate from per-vital slopes normalized to population standard deviations per day; projection re-runs the full Pulse pipeline at linearly extrapolated severities for 7, 14 and 30 days.

\subsection{Service, alerts and continuous state}
The FastAPI service exposes eleven endpoints over seven tables: patients, clinical reports, wearable readings, simulation runs and risk assessments, plus two tables for the continuous mode (saved engine states and state resets). The service persists all results, so every read endpoint is a database read and never blocks on the engine; in a timing test of 50 calls per endpoint, the five read endpoints answered in a median 2.7--4.2\,ms. Table~\ref{tab:stack} lists the technology stack.

Missing EF or NT-proBNP defaults to a documented healthy value (EF 62\%, NT-proBNP 100\,pg/mL), always flagged and never silently blended with measured values.

A continuous-state mode serializes the Pulse engine state after each day and resumes from it the next day, instead of rebuilding the patient from scratch (Section~\ref{sec:engineering}). Its first day applies only the EF- and severity-derived modifiers; each later day re-issues them and, for the two exertion labels (cardiac stress and acute deterioration), adds Pulse's \code{Exercise} action, which stays active in the saved state. The system applies the scenario-specific extras in Table~\ref{tab:scenarios} (venous compliance, heart-rate multiplier, systemic resistance and compliance) only in the default fresh mode. Both scenario-test experiments (Section~\ref{sec:results}) used the continuous mode.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:alert} gives the alert logic and Algorithm~\ref{alg:continuous} one day of the continuous-state twin. An engine run counts as \emph{unstable} if it failed, or if it was an acute-deterioration run with severity in the documented crash zone $[0.6,\,0.85]$, even when it completed; for unstable runs the alert falls back to the classifier's severity. On resume the engine cannot add or remove the \code{ChronicVentricularSystolicDysfunction} condition, so a new EF that crosses 40\% is reported as a mismatch until the twin is reset.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Daily alert decision with the C3 guard (\code{decide\_alert})}
\label{alg:alert}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Input:} classifier severity $\hat\sigma$; engine status $s$; today's risk bucket $b_t$, dominant mechanism $d_t$ and instability flag $F_{\text{inst},t}$; yesterday's streak $k_{t-1}$ and flag $\iota_{t-1}$.\\
\textbf{Output:} level $\in\{\text{ALERT},\text{WATCH},\text{NONE}\}$.
\begin{algsteps}
\item \textit{C3 bookkeeping, whenever a risk assessment is written:}
\item \kw{if} $b_t=\text{HIGH}$ \kw{and} $d_t=\text{baseline}$ \kw{then} $k_t\leftarrow k_{t-1}+1$; $\iota_t\leftarrow\iota_{t-1}$
\item \kw{else} $k_t\leftarrow0$; $\iota_t\leftarrow\text{false}$
\item \kw{if} $F_{\text{inst},t}=1$ \kw{then} $\iota_t\leftarrow\text{true}$
\item \kw{if} $s=\text{unstable}$ \kw{then} \kw{return} ALERT \kw{if} $\hat\sigma>0.15$ \kw{else} NONE \hfill\textit{classifier fallback}
\item \kw{if} $s=\text{valid}$ \kw{then}
\item \Ind\kw{if} $b_t=\text{HIGH}$ \kw{then}
\item \Ind\Ind\kw{if} $k_t>3$ \kw{and not} $\iota_t$ \kw{then} \kw{return} WATCH \hfill\textit{C3: downgrade}
\item \Ind\Ind\kw{return} ALERT
\item \Ind\kw{if} $b_t=\text{MODERATE}$ \kw{then} \kw{return} WATCH
\item \Ind\kw{return} NONE
\item \kw{return} NONE \hfill\textit{engine not yet run}
\end{algsteps}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{One day of the continuous-state twin (\code{run\_daily\_continuous\_pipeline})}
\label{alg:continuous}
\footnotesize
\textbf{Input:} patient's wearable readings and clinical reports; saved state $S_{t-1}$ (none on the first day or after a reset), with the EF used for it.\\
\textbf{Output:} saved state $S_t$; risk assessment and alert for day $t$.
\begin{algsteps}
\item $W\leftarrow$ last 21 days of wearable readings; \kw{if} fewer than 21 \kw{then} stop
\item \kw{if} a clinical report arrived after $S_{t-1}$ \kw{then} take EF and NT-proBNP from it
\item \kw{else} carry forward the EF stored with $S_{t-1}$ \hfill\textit{EF changes only with a new report}
\item \Ind missing EF/NT-proBNP $\rightarrow$ 62\%, 100\,pg/mL, flagged
\item $(\hat c,\hat\sigma)\leftarrow$ classifier and regressor on features of $W$ and clinical values
\item $M\leftarrow$ \code{CardiovascularMechanicsModification}$(m_{\text{SV}},m_R,m_C)$ from~(\ref{eq:deficit})--(\ref{eq:basemods})
\item \kw{if} $S_{t-1}$ is none \kw{then}
\item \Ind build the clamped patient file~(\ref{eq:clamp}); add the condition \kw{if} EF $\le40\%$
\item \Ind stabilize 60\,s; apply $M$; advance 600\,s
\item \kw{else}
\item \Ind load $S_{t-1}$ (\code{EngineStateFile}); re-issue $M$ \hfill\textit{state does not carry it}
\item \Ind \kw{if} $\hat c\in\{$cardiac stress, acute deterioration$\}$ \kw{then} apply \code{Exercise} at $\operatorname{clip}(f_{\hat c}\,\hat\sigma,0.1,0.5)$, $f=1.0$ or $0.6$
\item \Ind advance 600\,s
\item \kw{if} the engine failed \kw{then} record a failed run; keep $S_{t-1}$; alert by line~5 of Algorithm~\ref{alg:alert}; stop
\item $S_t\leftarrow$ \code{SerializeState}; store EF, $\hat\sigma$, simulation time and report id with $S_t$
\item compute~(\ref{eq:features})--(\ref{eq:risk}) on today's output; run Algorithm~\ref{alg:alert}
\end{algsteps}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Pulse instability at high severity}
In a 150-patient batch (30 per scenario), 117 runs completed (Fig.~\ref{fig:batch}). All 33 failures occurred in the two scenarios that add an \code{Exercise} action; 30 were engine crashes and 3 were timeouts, and failed patients had high severity (mean 0.75 and 0.72), so missingness is \emph{not at random} with respect to severity.

Engine logs for three independent crashes showed an identical chain: fatigue, hypoxia and tachycardia, then renal hypoperfusion and cardiovascular collapse, ending in a fatal negative heart-chamber volume and an irreversible-state event at 154.1--154.3\,s.

We applied single-variable interventions to a reproducible case (Table~\ref{tab:crashinv}). Gaps and ramps only delayed the crash; either stressor alone completed. The failure is therefore a sustained combined-load boundary whose safe exercise intensity varies by patient (about 0.22 for one patient and between 0.125 and 0.25 for another), so no single safe constant exists.

\subsection{Two failure mechanisms with opposite character}
Failures were not a single phenomenon. Mechanism 1 (engine crash) depends on the patient's own severity and is deterministic, so retrying an identical scenario does not help. Mechanism 2 (timeouts and connection-pool exhaustion) depended on how many other patients were running: at 9 workers, 5/11 patients hit connection timeouts and 6/11 hit the 180\,s engine timeout; at 4 workers 8/11 still timed out; at 2 workers there were no failures. A bare completion rate conflates the two; we report the mechanism with each rate.

\subsection{Continuous state: carrying a patient forward}
Rebuilding a patient every day discards the physiological state the twin is meant to track. Pulse can serialize its full state (about 2.33\,MB, saved in 0.31--0.44\,s and loaded in 0.43\,s) and resume it, and with an \code{Exercise} action the resumed run's final heart rate, MAP, cardiac output and stroke volume matched an uninterrupted run to every printed digit.

With our pipeline's disease modifier, they did not match (Table~\ref{tab:resume}): the serialized state does not carry the modifier. Re-issuing it on every resume restored the match, to 15 significant figures in all four outputs (we did not compare the serialized states themselves), which also requires storing the last-used EF and severity alongside the state. A rerun through the command-line driver used in production shows the same pattern over time (Fig.~\ref{fig:resume}).

\subsection{Other modeling corrections}
Three further corrections followed from running the real system rather than offline checks.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Severity (classifier output) and risk score (simulation-derived) share a $[0,1]$ range but not a scale: for acute deterioration the risk score's minimum (0.491) exceeded the severity mean (0.385). The projection module had applied a risk-score calibration to severity; we separated the two calibrations, and the system now reports the two quantities side by side, never fused.
\item The classifier's label occasionally flipped between days for genuinely ambiguous trajectories; a categorical persistence rule (window chosen by sweep) now stabilizes the reported scenario.
\item We tried adding rolling-window curvature features (``Option A''). They kept accuracy unchanged against a production model retrained in the same environment (90.7\% both) but worsened severity MAE from 0.0473 to 0.0506 on held-out data, so we reverted them.
\end{enumerate}

We evaluate in layers of increasing realism. This section reports the layers that use synthetic patients; Section~\ref{sec:validation} reports the tests against real data. Fig.~\ref{fig:evaldesign} shows the design, and Table~\ref{tab:evalsummary} summarizes what each layer tests and what it does not. We report the following scenarios, in this order:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Offline model test} (Section~\ref{sec:offline}): 300 held-out synthetic patients.
\item \emph{Engine scenario check} (Section~\ref{sec:enginecheck}): one simulated patient per scenario at severity $\approx$0.5, and the 117 completed batch runs.
\item \emph{Live API validation} (Section~\ref{sec:live}): 25, then 50 attempted synthetic patients replayed day by day through the live API.
\item \emph{Pre-specified 21-day scenario test, Experiment~1} (Section~\ref{sec:exp1}): ten scripted stories on development seeds 42--44 and held-out seeds 45--47; on the same runs, an ablation of the alert definition (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) and an analysis of false alerts and label errors (Section~\ref{sec:whyfa}).
\item \emph{Experiment~2} (Section~\ref{sec:exp2}): the same stories with ejection fraction 58--67\% on new seeds 48--53, followed by the mechanism behind high-risk days in both experiments (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
\end{itemize}
The metrics are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Accuracy} and \emph{severity MAE}: the share of held-out patients assigned the correct scenario, and the mean absolute error of predicted severity on its 0--1 scale.
\item \emph{Label accuracy}: the share of scenario-test patient-days on which the predicted scenario matches the story's intended scenario.
\item \emph{Sensitivity}: deteriorating patient-seed series (five stories $\times$ six seeds $=30$) with at least one alert on or after the story's onset day; \emph{strict detection} also requires the first alert to fall on or after onset.
\item \emph{Pre-onset alerts per 100 days}: alert days before onset in the deteriorating series, per 100 such days.
\item \emph{False alerts per 100 patient-days} (FA/100\,pd): alert days in the three stay-quiet stories (18 series), per 100 of their patient-days; \emph{specificity} counts stay-quiet series with no alert at all.
\item \emph{AUC}: area under the ROC curve for real outcomes, with bootstrap 95\% CIs (Section~\ref{sec:outcomes}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Offline model accuracy}\label{sec:offline}
On the 300-patient held-out test set, the classifier reached 91.3\% accuracy (274/300, bootstrap 95\% CI 88.0--94.3\%; macro-F1 0.91) and the regressor severity MAE 0.047 (RMSE 0.061). Per-class precision and recall were 0.88--0.95, with no class collapsed into another. The earlier 92.3\% figure belonged to a version that included NYHA class, which we removed for the reason in Table~\ref{tab:problems}.

Because test patients come from the same generator, these figures measure how well the model inverts the generator, not how it performs on independent trajectories; the pre-specified cohort below is the more relevant estimate (17.6\% day-by-day label accuracy with the cohort's EF band below the training range, 37.1\% with it inside).

Cardiac stress (preserved EF) and acute deterioration (reduced EF), the pair hardest to separate from simulated HR/MAP alone, were cleanly separated because EF is an input. Removing all 24 wearable features cost 40 points of accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), confirming that the wearable trend, not the clinical snapshot, carries most of the signal.

\subsection{Engine-level scenario check}\label{sec:enginecheck}
We simulated each scenario for one patient at severity $\approx$0.5 (Table~\ref{tab:phase2}). Responses were physiologically distinct. The clearest contrast is between cardiac stress and acute deterioration: both raised heart rate, but the healthy heart (EF 67\%) raised cardiac output from 5,910 to 9,948\,mL/min with stroke volume maintained, while the failing heart (EF 26.6\%) could not increase stroke volume (63$\rightarrow$67\,mL) and MAP fell to 52\,mmHg. We did not tune this contrast; it emerged from the EF-driven modifiers.

Across the 117 completed batch runs, outputs also followed the severity given to the engine where each scenario's mechanism predicts (Spearman $\rho$): heart rate (0.92 and 0.89) and cardiac output (0.81 and 0.92) rose with severity in cardiac stress and acute deterioration, and MAP fell with it in cardiac stress ($-0.73$); stable runs showed no dose response, as designed, and fluid-overload MAP barely moved ($\rho=0.15$), consistent with that scenario's missing volume-loading mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

The engine's left-heart pressure--volume signal was also physiologically plausible (volume 62.2--143.0\,mL, pressure 5.8--136.8\,mmHg at EF 45\%), and the dashboard shows it as a PV loop. Fig.~\ref{fig:pvecg} shows loops recorded during the scenario test for the same simulated patient before and after deterioration: by day 14 the loop has shifted to larger volumes (end-diastolic volume from about 150 to 220\,mL) with a lower peak pressure (about 139 to 121\,mmHg). The ECG beside it is a stored template that Pulse scales with heart rate; it does not respond to EF or scenario and is displayed for reference only.

\subsection{Live API validation}\label{sec:live}
We replayed twenty-five synthetic patients (five per scenario) day by day through the live API with no mocking: 25/25 completed and all 25 were classified correctly, but severity MAE was 0.271, far from that model's offline 0.048 (the version that still used NYHA class; the production model's offline MAE is 0.047). Per-patient inspection showed predictions compressed into a narrow 0.02--0.19 band, which we traced to the NYHA train/inference skew.

After we removed the feature and retrained, live re-validation reached severity MAE 0.008 on five patients and, expanded to 50 attempted (45 completed), MAE 0.0264 (95\% bootstrap CI 0.0194--0.0352) with 100\% scenario accuracy. The five failures were all high-severity exertion scenarios, matching Mechanism~1.

\subsubsection{Two alert definitions}
Our first analysis alerted on the classifier's severity ($>0.15$) and caught all five deteriorating patients, but also alerted on all three stable ones (58.7 false alerts per 100 patient-days). A code audit then showed that the dashboard did not use this signal at all: it displayed the risk-score bucket (HIGH). When we re-analysed under the dashboard's definition, the system detected three of five deteriorating patients (P06, P07, P10) with 18.0 false alerts per 100 patient-days.

P05 (deconditioning) produced near-zero values on every risk component on all 63 patient-days, and P04 (fluid overload) did not congest in some seeds, so neither had any signal for an alert rule to act on.

\subsubsection{Alert rule selection}
We specified three candidate rules before computing any of them (Table~\ref{tab:alerts}). C3 downgrades a sustained HIGH that is driven only by the baseline-deficit term with no corroborating instability, the diagnosed cause of P08's persistent false alarm. It kept all three detections and cut false alerts by 67.6\% on development seeds and 61.4\% on held-out seeds, where we never tuned it.

\subsubsection{Unified alert decision}
We then merged the two alert signals into a single \code{decide\_alert()} function, now the only source of alert state in the API and dashboard. It has three levels: ALERT (risk bucket HIGH and not downgraded by C3, or a crashed/failed engine run via the classifier-severity fallback); WATCH (risk bucket MODERATE, or a HIGH streak that C3 downgrades); and NONE. C3 therefore downgrades but never silences: a HIGH risk bucket can never map to NONE.

Replaying the saved scenario-test runs through the deployed functions reproduced the detection pattern on both seed sets (P06, P07 and P10 at ALERT in every seed; P01 and P09 quiet in every seed). P08's false alarm, previously ALERT for 8--14 consecutive days, fell to about 3 ALERT days per seed, with the remaining elevated days shown as WATCH. For P04 held-out seed 47, which never reached HIGH, 20 of 21 days now read WATCH, making a previously invisible fluid-overload trajectory visible for the first time.

\subsection{Ablation: does the physiology layer improve alerting?}\label{sec:ablation}
The premise of the twin is that simulating physiology adds something the classifier alone does not. We tested this on the same saved runs by changing only the alert definition (Table~\ref{tab:ablation2}, Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}). At its default threshold (severity above the stable range, 0.15) the classifier detected all 30 deteriorating series but alerted in every stable series, and passing its output through Pulse and the C3 guard reduced false alerts from 60.1 to 8.7 per 100 patient-days at a sensitivity of 70\%. Requiring the first alert to follow onset lowers this to 14/30: P07, whose onset is on day 10, alerted before onset in all six seeds.

That comparison, however, favors the physiology layer, because we never tuned the classifier's threshold. Sweeping the classifier's severity threshold traces a curve that lies above and to the left of every Pulse-based rule (Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}): at 0.55 it detected 30/30 series with 8.5 false alerts per 100 patient-days, and at 0.60 it detected 28/30 with 2.4.

A rule using only the predicted label (alert on an exertion scenario) performed similarly to the Pulse risk bucket (19/30 series, 18.8 false alerts), consistent with the label, more than the simulated physiology, determining when Pulse reports high risk.

To avoid tuning on the test data, we selected the classifier threshold on development seeds alone, using a rule fixed in advance (the lowest threshold whose development false-alert rate does not exceed the deployed system's, 5.8), exactly as we selected C3, and evaluated it once on held-out seeds (Table~\ref{tab:fairbase}). The rule chose 0.55. On held-out seeds the classifier detected all 15 deteriorating series and the deployed Pulse system 10, at the same false-alert rate (11.6 per 100 patient-days). In this case study, therefore, the physiology layer did not improve alerting over a properly thresholded classifier.

A conventional weight rule ($\geq2$\,kg within 3 days) never false-alerted but detected only 2/30 series, because the scripted fluid build-up (about 0.4\,kg per day) never reaches 2\,kg within three days.

\subsubsection{Absolute burden and sample size}
Even the best rules carry a clinically heavy absolute rate: 8.7 false alerts per 100 patient-days means roughly one false alert every 11 or 12 days for each stable patient, and one every 9 days on the held-out seeds alone.

With ten stories and six seeds, the confidence intervals are wide (Table~\ref{tab:ablation2}); we present these results as a controlled case study, not as estimates of performance in a patient population. Engine failures did not distort them: only 3 of 1,260 patient-days failed, and every metric was identical whether we scored failed days as alerts (the deployed fallback) or as no alert.

\subsection{Why false alerts occur}\label{sec:whyfa}
Feature importances show the severity regressor relies on resting-HR delta and slope (63.2\% combined) and step-count trends (27.2\%), the signals that ordinary day-to-day variation moves most, while EF ranks tenth (0.6\%). Adding Gaussian noise at one population SD to a stable patient's window moved predicted severity from 0.11 to 0.43, crossing the stable-range threshold (Table~\ref{tab:noise}).

\textbf{The classifier mostly predicted the wrong scenario.} Day-by-day scenario-label accuracy on this cohort was 17.6\% (222/1,260 patient-days; 19.8\% if days before a story's onset are scored as ``stable''), below the 20\% expected from guessing among five classes and far below the 91.3\% in-distribution test accuracy. The confusion matrix (Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion}) shows why: ``deconditioning'' was predicted on 71.5\% of all days, including 480 of 630 days of stable stories and 113 of 126 days of the fluid-overload story; acute deterioration was recognized on only 11 days.

A likely cause is distribution shift: the cohort's neutral EF band (48--57\%) sits below the EF range of the training ``stable'' class (51.3--70.7\%) and near the deconditioning mean; Experiment~2 tests this explanation directly.

In the continuous mode used here, the label changes the simulation only through exertion (Section~\ref{sec:methods}): stable, fluid-overload and deconditioning labels produce the same simulation at a given EF and severity. The label errors therefore mattered where they added an exertion label to a story without one or withheld it from a story that needed it. The risk score still reached HIGH on 28\% of deconditioning-labeled days (255/898) and 83\% of cardiac-stress-labeled days (185/224); once exertion days have occurred, the carried-forward engine state keeps the score high on later days even when the label reverts to deconditioning (Fig.~\ref{fig:timelines}; quantified in Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). The ablation results therefore describe the pipeline as it behaves with these label errors, not a physiology layer driven by correct scenarios.

\subsection{Experiment 2: the same stories with an in-distribution EF band}\label{sec:exp2}
To test the distribution-shift explanation, we repeated the scenario test with three changes and nothing else, recorded with predictions for every outcome before any run (commit \code{f353bd0}): the cohort's EF band moved from 48--57\% to 58--67\%, inside the training ``stable'' range; P07's onset moved from day 10 to day 4, like the other stories; and one unscored warm-up day was added so that every scored day, including day 1, is a full simulation day. We left the models, risk score, thresholds, C3 guard and feature flags unchanged and used six new seeds (48--53). The pre-specified plan also disclosed a classifier-only dry run on development seeds and fixed sensitivity and pre-onset alert rate as co-primary outcomes.

All 60 series completed (3 of 1,260 patient-days failed, as in Experiment 1). Table~\ref{tab:exp2} compares both experiments. Label accuracy rose from 17.6\% to 37.1\% (468/1,260; 41.5\% with pre-onset days scored as ``stable''), and deconditioning fell from 71.5\% to 52.9\% of days.

The deployed alert caught 24/30 deteriorating series (21/30 before), alerted on 10.0 per 100 pre-onset days (31.7 before), and its false alerts fell from 8.7 to 4.8 per 100 patient-days, with P01 and P09 now silent in every seed.

The results met six of the eight outcome predictions. Two were not met: strict detection rose to 17/30 instead of falling, because the number of series alerting before onset stayed at 7/30 rather than rising as predicted; and the classifier's false alerts at 0.55 were 10.1 per 100 patient-days, just above the predicted ceiling of 10.

The classifier still never labelled fluid overload (P04) or acute deterioration (P07) correctly: in the training data those classes occur only at EF $\leq$48\%, so at the new EF band acute deterioration was predicted on only 8 days (all P10) and fluid overload on none.

The main finding is unchanged: the classifier at its frozen threshold of 0.55 still detected all 30 series, each with its first alert on or after onset, while the Pulse-based alert, now with fewer false alerts, missed six.

\subsection{How the twin's high-risk days arise}\label{sec:mechanism}
The saved runs of both experiments show one mechanism, replicated on new seeds and a new EF band; we found it in a descriptive analysis after both experiments. No HIGH-risk day preceded a series' first exertion label (0 of 462 HIGH days in Experiment~1, 0 of 475 in Experiment~2). Series with an exertion label reached HIGH in 30 of 32 and 30 of 31 cases, series without one in 0 of 28 and 0 of 29, and the first HIGH day came 0--1 days after the first exertion label in 23 of 30 series in each experiment.

The exertion label starts Pulse's \code{Exercise} action, which stays active in the carried state: later days without an exertion label started at a median MAP of 66\,mmHg, against 95\,mmHg before any exertion label (Fig.~\ref{fig:engineoutput}), and the baseline-deficit term, which scores starting MAP, decided 452 of 462 and 471 of 475 HIGH days (Fig.~\ref{fig:riskbreakdown}). In the continuous mode, HIGH risk therefore reports an exertion state that the label switches on and that then persists, rather than a gradual change in the simulated circulation.

\section{Validation}\label{sec:validation}
%=====================================================================
We tested the pipeline, or the components that can run without a 21-day wearable window, against three kinds of real data: home measurements (Section~\ref{sec:perheart}), measured hemodynamics (Section~\ref{sec:bcg}) and recorded outcomes (Section~\ref{sec:outcomes}).

\subsection{Real-patient replay (PerHeart)}\label{sec:perheart}
The PerHeart pilot dataset \cite{kolakowski2026perheart} contains one month of real home measurements (pulse oximeter, scale, blood-pressure cuff) from 27 HF patients aged 67--94. Sixteen had at least 21 real overlapping days of heart rate, SpO$_2$ and weight; we fabricated no day, and unmeasured fields (sleep, HRV, steps, EF, NT-proBNP) took documented defaults with explicit flags. Every patient required the engine's age proxy.

Before the severity fix, all 16 completed but severities were compressed (0.078--0.148) and no patient reached HIGH, independently reproducing the NYHA skew on real data. After the fix, severities spread (0.093--0.516) and risk buckets were 4 LOW, 4 MODERATE and 5 HIGH (previously 4 LOW, 12 MODERATE, 0 HIGH), while three patients now crashed with the deterministic engine signature.

The system staged all 16 as NYHA~I, an artifact of the identical healthy EF/BNP defaults rather than a clinical finding. The one fluid-overload patient scored LOW because the EF default makes Pulse simulate a structurally normal heart, so the baseline-deficit term cannot fire; the API now names this mechanism in its caveat.

We searched the literature for a BNP-to-EF proxy and found none: the clinical relationship runs from EF to BNP, not the reverse.



\subsection{Measured hemodynamics (BCG, two patients)}\label{sec:bcg}
For two patients from a ballistocardiography dataset with echocardiographic volumes \cite{zhan2025bcg}, we used BCG features to adjust the twin's vascular compliance and compared simulated stroke volume and cardiac output with measured values (Table~\ref{tab:bcg}). Simulated cardiac output was 45--46\% below measured in both; 71.3\% of patient 14's stroke-volume shortfall was already present from anthropometrics alone, before EF or BCG data entered the model.

For a synthetic 21-day deterioration anchored to these patients' real HR and EF, the classifier eventually assigned the correct label for both, but labeled patient 14 as fluid overload for days 6--10 before converging.

\subsection{Real-outcome tests}\label{sec:outcomes}
Two real cohorts lack the 21-day wearable window and EF needed to run the whole twin, so we tested only components with a non-fabricated input path (Fig.~\ref{fig:auc}, Table~\ref{tab:auc}). The baseline-deficit term is a function of starting MAP alone; applied to measured first-24-hour MAP in 17,129 MIMIC-IV HF ICU admissions, it predicted in-hospital mortality with AUC 0.596 (95\% CI 0.585--0.608), with mortality rising from 8.9\% to 21.2\% across score deciles; age alone gave 0.591 (0.579--0.602) on the same admissions. This shows that low admission MAP carries modest prognostic information in an ICU population, about as much as age; it is not a test of the twin, which we did not run on these patients.

On the Zigong cohort \cite{zhang2021zigong} (6-month death or readmission), the same term reached 0.548 (0.525--0.573) on all 2,001 cleaned admissions but 0.533 (0.490--0.575) on the 625 complete cases, and a clinical-only variant of the scenario model 0.517 (0.473--0.562): weak signal at best.

As external context, published LACE-index AUCs \cite{vanwalraven2010lace} lie around 0.56--0.65 in other populations and outcomes (not directly comparable), an 11-of-13-variable MAGGIC score (point table checked against the published integer-score chart \cite{pocock2013maggic}) reached 0.604 on this cohort, and a model using laboratory panels reached 0.667, signal carried by renal and coagulation tests that a wearable-based system cannot obtain.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
%=====================================================================
\subsection{What the twin does well}
The pipeline runs end to end, day after day, on synthetic and real home data, and continuous state makes it a persistent model of a patient, whose resumed outputs match an uninterrupted run, rather than a fresh simulation each day.

Its scenarios produce physiologically distinct responses through mechanism rather than labels (Table~\ref{tab:phase2}), and in the exertion scenarios its outputs follow severity.

Because every alert decomposes into simulated hemodynamic components, we could diagnose and guard against a specific false-alarm pattern: the C3 guard cut false alerts by 61--68\% without losing a detection, and it transferred unchanged to held-out seeds.

The evaluation also shows that we understand the system's behavior well enough to predict it: Experiment~2 met six of the eight outcome predictions we committed before its runs, and the exertion mechanism replicated on new seeds and a new EF band.

\subsection{Is the mechanistic layer circular?}
For detection, largely, in its current form. The classifier's label selects the Pulse scenario and its severity scales it, and in the continuous mode every HIGH-risk day followed an exertion label, so the simulated physiology re-expresses the classifier's decision rather than observing new evidence. The ablation agrees: a label-only rule behaves like the Pulse risk bucket, and a classifier threshold tuned on development seeds matched the deployed system's held-out false-alert rate while detecting more deteriorating series.

What the engine adds is different in kind: a hemodynamic account of each alert (pressure--volume loops, cardiac output, compensation), the component structure that made the C3 guard possible, and persistent state. We have not yet tested the value of these explanations to clinicians.

Two factors limit what the engine could add to detection here. First, the label was wrong on most days in Experiment~1; moving EF into the training range (Experiment~2) roughly doubled label accuracy and reduced false alerts, but did not close the gap to the tuned classifier. Second, the scenario that should carry congestion (fluid overload) is mis-specified.

Showing a detection benefit would require correct exertion labels, simulated exertion that ends when the label no longer calls for it, a corrected fluid-overload scenario, and ideally physiological inputs (e.g., measured weight-driven volume changes) that do not pass through the classifier.

\subsection{How personal is the twin?}
Less than the term usually implies. On real patients, every PerHeart body was an age proxy, ejection fraction and NT-proBNP were defaults, simulated cardiac output was 45--46\% below measured in the two BCG patients, and the severity regressor relies on resting-HR and step trends for about 90\% of its importance (EF: 0.6\%). The current system is best described as a population-parameterized physiological model driven by an individual's wearable trends; per-patient calibration of baseline hemodynamics is the main step toward a personal twin.

\subsection{What it does not yet do}
Five findings bound the claims. First, two of five deterioration types are invisible to the current alert because their signatures never reach the simulated hemodynamics: deconditioning by design (no acute event), and fluid overload because the scenario reduces venous compliance without adding circulating volume, which mobilizes pooled blood rather than overloading the circulation. Second, the models are sensitive to ordinary wearable noise and to clinical inputs outside their training distribution. Third, on real outcomes, the only component testable without a wearable window (the baseline-deficit term) carries modest signal, comparable to age alone on MIMIC-IV and weak on Zigong; the full twin has not been tested against real events. Fourth, the physiology layer did not improve alerting over a tuned classifier. Fifth, even the best configurations produce roughly one false alert every 9--12 days per stable patient, a burden that would be hard to sustain clinically.

Our results therefore support the feasibility of running such a twin end to end, not an alerting benefit or clinical effectiveness.

\subsection{Lessons for building physiology-engine twins}
We found every major fault in Table~\ref{tab:problems} by running the real system, not by offline evaluation: the NYHA skew only appeared in live inference, the CORS fault only in a browser, the resume drift only with the production modifier, and the alert mismatch only by auditing what the dashboard actually displayed. Committing expectations before each run and holding out seeds guarded against post-hoc rationalization, and a pre-specified replication turned a diagnosis (distribution shift in EF) into a tested prediction. Reporting failure mechanisms rather than bare completion rates prevented a biased reading of the results.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item Training data are synthetic. Only the population statistics trace to real aggregate data; the healthy EF profile, wearable baselines and scenario changes are assumptions. The pre-specified cohort is small (ten stories, six seeds).
\item The fluid-overload scenario contains a modeling error rather than a mere limitation: it lowers venous compliance without adding circulating volume, which mobilizes pooled blood and moves the simulated hemodynamics in the improving direction as severity rises. Correcting it requires a volume-loading action, re-verification of engine stability, re-running the scenario validation, retraining the severity model and repeating the 21-day test; we have not yet done so.
\item We tuned several scoring elements post hoc (Table~\ref{tab:posthoc}).
\item The continuous mode simplifies each resumed day to the EF- and severity-derived modifiers plus exertion, without the scenario-specific extras; from its second day the risk features measure that day's change, whereas we designed the score on fresh runs (in both experiments the baseline term, which reads starting MAP rather than a within-day change, decided 452 of 462 and 471 of 475 HIGH days, so the within-day features set few HIGH days; we did not count how often they set MODERATE days); and in a demonstration check, two of five patients' risk rose over three resumed days with an unchanged label, a drift whose source we have not isolated.
\item Pulse's native range (age 18--65, BMI 16--30) excludes most HF patients, so we simulate older patients with a proxy body.
\item Real EF and NT-proBNP were unavailable in the replay cohort, and their defaults mask the fluid-overload mechanism.
\item The engine's SpO$_2$ output was unusable and its ECG is a template.
\item Pulse is unstable when disease modifiers combine with sustained exertion, which under-represents high-severity exertion scenarios in all datasets.
\item We do not model medication effects: Pulse includes a validated diuretic (furosemide), making diuretic modeling feasible, but has no beta-blocker or ACE-inhibitor/ARB substances.
\item Risk-bucket cut-points and the deterioration-rate calibration constants are engineering choices, not clinically validated thresholds.
\item The API has no authentication and is suitable only for research use.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
%=====================================================================
We built and evaluated HF-PULSE, a digital twin for HF home monitoring that interprets daily wearable trends through a mechanistic engine, scores the simulated hemodynamics with an interpretable risk score and, in its continuous mode, carries the engine's state from day to day. It runs end to end on synthetic and real home data, and we release it with frozen models, automated tests and a fresh-clone reproduction.

A pre-specified evaluation with held-out seeds and a replication showed what the physiology layer contributes and what it does not. Its component structure enabled an alert guard that cut false alerts by 61--68\%, including on unseen seeds; correcting the cohort's EF band doubled label accuracy and nearly halved false alerts, as predicted; and every high-risk day followed an exertion label. On held-out seeds, however, the classifier with a properly selected threshold detected all deteriorating series at the same false-alert rate at which the Pulse system detected two thirds, so the engine's present value is explanation rather than detection. The reasons are specific (label errors outside the training distribution, exertion that persists after the label changes, a mis-specified fluid-overload scenario, and dependence of the simulated scenario on the label) and therefore actionable.

Future work will:
\begin{enumerate}
\item fix the remaining scenario labels, through training data that cover fluid overload and acute deterioration at preserved EF or physiological inputs that bypass the label, end simulated exertion when the label no longer calls for it, and re-run the ablation;
\item add a volume-loading mechanism to the fluid-overload scenario and a diuretic action using Pulse's furosemide model;
\item systematically map the engine's stability boundary across severity, scenario and body profile;
\item make the models robust to wearable noise and out-of-distribution clinical inputs;
\item personalize the twin's baseline heart rate and hemodynamics from measured data; and
\item evaluate day-by-day early warning on real longitudinal data with recorded HF events, the test that ultimately decides clinical value.
\end{enumerate}
